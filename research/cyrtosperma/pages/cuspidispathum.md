TITLE: CYRTOSPERMA CUSPIDISPATHUM
AUTHORITY: Alderw.
TAGS: Cyrtosperma, Oceania, New Guinea, Indonesia, Papua, Papua New Guinea, Sandaun, East Sepik, Madang, Western Province, Southern Highlands, Gulf Province
PHOTOS: hero from inat/cuspidispathum (wild plants, Indonesian Papua, with the dangling twisted spathe tip); plates: Hay 1988 Figs 7a–7b; type sheet L 0041893 (Gjellerup 63)

PROTOLOGUE:
Cyrtosperma cuspidispathum Alderw., published in Bulletin du Jardin Botanique de Buitenzorg sér. 3, 1: 374 (1920), in "New or noteworthy Malayan Araceae II".
Type: Gjellerup 63, Papua, Eta River, upper Tami, 2 April 1910, "shady clayey river side; alt. 85 m" (holotype B, not seen by Hay; isotype L).
Translated from protologue: Petiole about 75 cm long, dark olive- and purple-flamed, sparsely and shortly prickly; blade hastate or sagittate-hastate, about 50 cm long, the anterior lobe oblong, about 20 by 7 cm, abruptly and slenderly beaked at the tip rather than acuminate, the posterior lobes obliquely sublanceolate, about 30 by 10 cm, parted by a right-angled sinus, the posterior costae slightly decurved, bare in the sinus for 0.5–1 cm, unarmed beneath. Peduncle about 85 cm, sparsely prickly below and rather copiously so towards the top. Spathe about 35 cm long, dark violet, sparsely veined, the lower part long-oblong, about 18 by 4 cm, abruptly narrowed into a linear cusp, sharply subulate towards the tip and about as long as the lower part. Spadix stipitate, the stipe about 1 cm, cylindric, about 7.5 cm long and 6 mm thick, greenish white; tepals and stamens six; ovaries cylindric, flattened at the base, rounded and truncate at the apex, one-ovulate.² Alderwerelt added that the New Guinea plants Engler had determined as C. merkusii belong to three species, this one, C. subulispathum and C. hastatum.²

SYNONYMS:
HOMOTYPIC SYNONYMS: N/A
HETEROTYPIC SYNONYMS: N/A. Engler's New Guinea records of Cyrtosperma merkusii and C. edule (1879, 1911) and Krause & Alderwerelt's C. macrotum (1924, as to specimens cited) belong here in part.⁴
ACCEPTED INFRASPECIFICS: N/A

DISTRIBUTION:
New Guinea: New Guinea.¹
DISTRIBUTION NOTE: Indonesian Papua (Jayapura: Eta River) and Papua New Guinea in Sandaun (West Sepik), East Sepik, Madang, Western, Southern Highlands and Gulf Provinces.⁴ Herbarium records span 24 to about 760 m, median c. 300 m.⁵

SPECIES DESCRIPTION:
Moderately robust to gigantic, solitary or rarely suckering herb to c. 4 m tall; rhizome creeping, subterranean, up to 13 cm in diameter but very short because the older parts rot quickly. Leaves 2 to several; petiole brown, green, or mottled in combinations of these with white; armature sparse to dense, the spines, at least in the lower parts of the petioles and peduncles, small, squat, abruptly upturned and easily dislodged; petioles standing subparallel, not distinctly diverging from the base; blade hastate or sagittate, to 1.3 m long, in smaller plants often with the lobes much narrower than long, held with the posterior lobes down and the anterior lobe up, somewhat flaccid, unarmed.⁴

INFLORESCENCE:
Peduncle somewhat shorter than the petioles. Spathe 17 to c. 100 cm long, clasping in the lower quarter, ovate-lanceolate, in smaller plants erect, with increasing size the apical acuminate portion somewhat reflexed to greatly elongated, spirally twisted and dangling, purple-brown, usually with yellow veins outside and with pale pink to cream streaks towards the midline within; spadix to 18 cm long in flower, with a free, sometimes armed stipe 2–6 cm long. Flowers hexamerous; anthers not exceeding the tepals at male anthesis; stigmas more or less sessile, white turning brown; ovaries predominantly uniovulate, rarely biovulate. Fruit when ripe expelled from the spadix and held there by the stripped-away adaxial epidermis of the tepals. Seed slightly and irregularly ridged.⁴

ECOLOGY:
Lowland rainforest undergrowth and regrowth, predominantly on alluvial soils; one collection from lower montane forest on limestone at 500–600 m.⁴ In the Gogol Valley plants in logged regrowth reached the maximum size recorded for the species seven years after clearing, and seedlings establish in one-year-old regrowth.⁴

DISTRIBUTION MAPS:
Hay 1988 Fig. 8.

ETYMOLOGY:
From the Latin cuspis, a point, and spatha: the spathe drawn out into a long, tapering cusp.

NOTES:
1. One of the giants of the genus, with C. giganteum and C. timikense; Hay & Imran (2020) compare their C. timikense with this species, which it most resembles.⁶
2. Flowering is protogynous. The stigmas become receptive shortly after the spathe unrolls, in a rapid basipetal sequence over about two days, and remain receptive for about three weeks, each bearing a drop of nectar, unless pollination has occurred. Pollen is released only after the stigmas have ceased; the anthers open by apical pore-like slits and extrude the pollen in coherent rope-like masses, all the anthers of a flower together and in a slow basipetal sequence lasting several weeks along the spadix, the larger the inflorescence the longer the anthesis.⁴
3. Once the nectariferous stigmas stop there is no reward but pollen. Weevils, Trochorhopalus strangulatus, a species ranging from the Indian to the Pacific Ocean, feed on the tissue inside the base of the spathe. The inflorescence emits a faint musty, fruity odour day and night before, during and shortly after anthesis.⁴
4. As the inflorescence becomes an infructescence the geniculus at the top of the peduncle turns the spadix from pointing vertically up to pointing vertically down.⁴
5. Ripe fruits are pushed out of the spadix, presumably by the pressure of their own growth, and dangle from it by the inner epidermis of the tepals, which is stripped away as they emerge; they recall arillate seeds hanging by their funicles. In most Cyrtosperma the fruits stay attached until removed or until the spadix disintegrates; the mechanism is otherwise familiar in Anthurium.⁴
6. The New Guinea specimens Engler cited under C. merkusii and C. edule were not located by Hay and may have been destroyed in Berlin, but from their localities they are almost certainly this species; Engler's Pflanzenreich fig. 6, captioned C. edule, is clearly this plant.⁴
7. In cultivation at Lae the rhizomes suckered into large clumps, which Hay did not see in the wild.⁴ Indonesian growers now cultivate it, and it appears among iNaturalist records as garden plants.³
